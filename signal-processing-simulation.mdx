A pressure sensor on a pump line gives a reading that jumps by half a bar from one sample to the next. An alarm trips when nothing is wrong, and a controller that follows the reading moves its valve all day. A filter gives a smooth line, but every filter pays for it with delay. Filter too hard and a burst pipe is shown late, or a short pressure spike is not shown at all. In this lesson you design a filter in Python, where you can see what it removes and what it delays, and then move its numbers to C for a microcontroller. #SignalProcessing #DigitalFilters #EmbeddedSystems

## Digital Filter Fundamentals

<Card title="The Core Idea" icon="star">
A digital filter is a small calculation done on every new reading: it combines the newest reading, $x[n]$, and some earlier ones, $x[n-1]$, $x[n-2]$, into one output value, $y[n]$. Here $n$ counts the readings. You can describe the same filter by its difference equation (the calculation itself), by its frequency response (which frequencies pass and which are weakened), or by its response to one sudden change (how late the output is).
</Card>

There are two families. A FIR filter (finite impulse response) uses only readings. An IIR filter (infinite impulse response) also uses its own earlier outputs, as a running total does.

```text
  FIR: readings only
  x[n] ──► [b0] ──┐
  x[n-1] ► [b1] ──┤
  x[n-2] ► [b2] ──┼──► y[n] = weighted readings
  x[n-M] ► [bM] ──┘    (always stable)

  IIR: readings and earlier outputs
  x[n] ──► [b0] ──┐
  x[n-1] ► [b1] ──┤
                  ├──► y[n] = weighted readings
  y[n-1] ► [a1] ──┤           - weighted earlier outputs
  y[n-2] ► [a2] ──┘    (feedback, can be unstable)
```

### FIR vs IIR Filters

| Property | FIR | IIR |
|----------|------|------|
| Uses earlier outputs? | No | Yes |
| Stability | Always stable | Can be unstable |
| Delay | Can be the same at all frequencies | Changes with frequency |
| Size for the same sharpness | Large (many stored readings, called taps) | Small (few coefficients) |
| Example | Moving average | Butterworth, Chebyshev |

IIR filters are often chosen for microcontrollers, where memory and time are short.

### The Difference Equation

In words: multiply the newest reading and the earlier readings by fixed numbers $b_k$ and add them. Then subtract the earlier outputs, each multiplied by a fixed number $a_k$:

$$y[n] = \frac{1}{a_0}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k \cdot x[n-k] - \sum_{k=1}^{N} a_k \cdot y[n-k]\right)$$

$M$ and $N$ are the numbers of earlier readings and earlier outputs used. A FIR filter has no $a_k$ terms. SciPy's `signal.butter` gives you exactly these $b$ and $a$ coefficients.

## Moving Average Filter

<InArticleAd />


The simplest FIR filter replaces each reading by the mean of the most recent $M$ readings:

$$y[n] = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=0}^{M-1} x[n-k]$$

It is a low-pass filter: slow changes pass and fast changes are weakened. Random noise falls by a factor of $\sqrt{M}$, so 50 readings give about 7 times less noise.

The price is delay. After a sudden real change the mean still holds old readings, so it shows 90% of the change only after $0.9M$ readings: about 45 ms at 1,000 readings per second and $M = 50$. An event shorter than the window is treated worse: a 20 ms pressure spike is shown at 40% of its height. So choose $M$ from the fastest real change you must see, not from how smooth the line looks.

The moving average separates signal from noise only gently. It removes completely the frequencies that are multiples of $f_s / M$, where $f_s$ is the sample rate in Hz. For the mathematics of frequencies and the FFT, see [Applied Mathematics: Fourier Analysis and the Frequency Domain](/education/applied-mathematics/fourier-analysis-frequency-domain/).

**When to use it:** quick noise reduction where a sharp cutoff is not needed. It is easy to write on any microcontroller (MCU): a buffer of the last $M$ readings and a running sum.

## Butterworth Filter

<InArticleAd />


The Butterworth filter is the usual first choice for sensor signals. Below its cutoff frequency it passes the signal almost unchanged, with no ripple. Above the cutoff its gain (output size divided by input size) falls steadily. It is an IIR filter set by two numbers:

- **Cutoff frequency** $f_c$ in Hz: the frequency at which the gain is 70.7%, written $-3$ dB. The decibel (dB) is a way to write a ratio: $-20$ dB means 10 times smaller.
- **Order** $N$: how steeply the gain falls above the cutoff. Each order adds 20 dB per decade, which is 10 times weaker for every 10 times in frequency. A higher order needs more calculation and gives more delay.

A 4th-order Butterworth falls at 80 dB per decade: a signal at 10 times the cutoff frequency comes out 10,000 times weaker. For most sensor signals, order 2 to 4 is enough.

### Designing with SciPy

```
scipy.signal.butter(N, Wn, btype, fs)
```

- `N`: filter order
- `Wn`: cutoff frequency in Hz (when `fs` is specified)
- `btype`: 'low', 'high', 'bandpass', 'bandstop'
- `fs`: sample rate in Hz

This returns `(b, a)` coefficient arrays that you can use directly in `scipy.signal.lfilter` or implement as a difference equation in C.

## Resampling and Decimation

<InArticleAd />


Sometimes the ADC (analog-to-digital converter, the part that turns a voltage into a number) runs much faster than you need. An ADC that samples at 10 kHz for a temperature loop that updates at 100 Hz gives 100 times more data than necessary. Decimation lowers the sample rate by keeping every $M$-th sample.

The trap: a signal sampled $f_s$ times a second can carry frequencies only up to $f_s / 2$, the Nyquist frequency. Anything faster still in the signal when you drop samples does not vanish: it comes back as a false slow signal, an alias, that no later filter can remove. A wheel in a film that seems to turn slowly backwards is the same effect. So filter first, then drop samples, as the program below does:

```text
  ┌──────────────┐     ┌──────────────┐     ┌──────────────┐
  │ 1,000 Hz     │────►│ low-pass     │────►│ keep every   │
  │ readings     │     │ below 40 Hz  │     │ 10th reading │
  └──────────────┘     └──────────────┘     └──────────────┘
    carries up to        anti-alias           100 Hz readings,
    500 Hz               filter               carry up to 50 Hz
```

## Project: Noise Filter Designer

<InArticleAd />


<Card title="What You Will Build" icon="rocket">
A filter design tool. It makes a noisy sensor signal whose true value is known, applies a moving average and a Butterworth low-pass filter, and plots the result against time and against frequency. Because the true value is known, it can print how far each filtered signal is from it. It also prints the coefficients in a form you can copy into C.
</Card>

### Complete Runnable Code

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import signal

# ============================================================
# Noise Filter Designer for Sensor Data
# ============================================================
# Design filters in Python, export coefficients for embedded C.

np.random.seed(42)

# --- Signal parameters ---
fs = 1000.0        # Sample rate (Hz) - typical ADC rate
duration = 2.0     # seconds
t = np.arange(0, duration, 1.0 / fs)
N = len(t)

# --- Generate a realistic sensor signal ---
# Slow-changing "true" signal: temperature ramp + small oscillation
true_signal = 25.0 + 3.0 * (t / duration) + 0.5 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 2 * t)

# Add realistic noise components:
# 1. White noise from ADC quantization and thermal noise
white_noise = 0.8 * np.random.randn(N)

# 2. 50/60 Hz power line interference
power_line_50 = 0.6 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 50 * t)
power_line_60 = 0.3 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 60 * t)

# 3. High-frequency switching noise (from a DC-DC converter nearby)
switching_noise = 0.4 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 200 * t + np.random.uniform(0, 2*np.pi))

# Combined noisy signal (what the ADC actually reads)
noisy_signal = true_signal + white_noise + power_line_50 + power_line_60 + switching_noise

# ============================================================
# Filter 1: Moving Average
# ============================================================
def moving_average(x, window_size):
    """Simple moving average filter."""
    kernel = np.ones(window_size) / window_size
    # This window is centred on each reading: it also uses later readings,
    # so it shows no delay. That is possible only on a finished recording.
    # Pad both ends with the edge values, then use 'valid' mode,
    # so the length is kept and the ends are not pulled toward zero
    left = window_size // 2
    right = window_size - 1 - left
    padded = np.pad(x, (left, right), mode='edge')
    filtered = np.convolve(padded, kernel, mode='valid')
    return filtered

ma_window = 50  # 50 samples at 1 kHz = 50 ms window
ma_filtered = moving_average(noisy_signal, ma_window)

# ============================================================
# Filter 2: Butterworth Low-Pass
# ============================================================
# Design: we want to keep signals below 10 Hz (our sensor changes slowly)
# and remove everything above (noise, power line, switching)
cutoff_hz = 10.0
filter_order = 4

b, a = signal.butter(filter_order, cutoff_hz, btype='low', fs=fs)
butter_filtered = signal.filtfilt(b, a, noisy_signal)  # zero-phase filtering

# Also apply with lfilter (causal, as it would run on an MCU)
butter_causal = signal.lfilter(b, a, noisy_signal)

# ============================================================
# Filter 3: Higher-order Butterworth for comparison
# ============================================================
b8, a8 = signal.butter(8, cutoff_hz, btype='low', fs=fs)
butter8_filtered = signal.filtfilt(b8, a8, noisy_signal)

# ============================================================
# Frequency domain analysis
# ============================================================
def compute_spectrum(x, fs):
    """Compute single-sided amplitude spectrum."""
    n = len(x)
    freqs = np.fft.rfftfreq(n, 1.0 / fs)
    magnitude = 2.0 / n * np.abs(np.fft.rfft(x))
    return freqs, magnitude

freqs_noisy, mag_noisy = compute_spectrum(noisy_signal, fs)
freqs_butter, mag_butter = compute_spectrum(butter_filtered, fs)

# Filter frequency response
w, h = signal.freqz(b, a, worN=2048, fs=fs)
w8, h8 = signal.freqz(b8, a8, worN=2048, fs=fs)

# ============================================================
# Compute errors
# ============================================================
rmse_noisy = np.sqrt(np.mean((noisy_signal - true_signal) ** 2))
rmse_ma = np.sqrt(np.mean((ma_filtered - true_signal) ** 2))
rmse_butter = np.sqrt(np.mean((butter_filtered - true_signal) ** 2))
rmse_butter8 = np.sqrt(np.mean((butter8_filtered - true_signal) ** 2))

print("=" * 60)
print("  Noise Filter Designer: Results")
print("=" * 60)
print(f"  Sample rate:          {fs:.0f} Hz")
print(f"  Signal duration:      {duration:.1f} s")
print(f"  Butterworth cutoff:   {cutoff_hz:.0f} Hz, order {filter_order}")
print("-" * 60)
print(f"  Raw noisy signal     RMSE: {rmse_noisy:.4f}")
print(f"  Moving average (M={ma_window:d})  RMSE: {rmse_ma:.4f}")
print(f"  Butterworth order 4  RMSE: {rmse_butter:.4f}")
print(f"  Butterworth order 8  RMSE: {rmse_butter8:.4f}")
print("=" * 60)

# ============================================================
# Export coefficients for C implementation
# ============================================================
print("\n--- C-ready filter coefficients ---")
print(f"// Butterworth low-pass, order {filter_order}, cutoff {cutoff_hz} Hz, fs {fs:.0f} Hz")
print(f"// Generated by scipy.signal.butter")
print(f"#define FILTER_ORDER {filter_order}")
print(f"#define NUM_B_COEFFS {len(b)}")
print(f"#define NUM_A_COEFFS {len(a)}")
print()
b_str = ", ".join(f"{coeff:.15e}" for coeff in b)
a_str = ", ".join(f"{coeff:.15e}" for coeff in a)
print(f"const double b[] = {{{b_str}}};")
print(f"const double a[] = {{{a_str}}};")
print()
print("// Difference equation implementation:")
print("// y[n] = b[0]*x[n] + b[1]*x[n-1] + ... + b[M]*x[n-M]")
print("//       - a[1]*y[n-1] - a[2]*y[n-2] - ... - a[N]*y[n-N]")
print("// (a[0] is always 1.0 for normalized coefficients)")

# ============================================================
# Decimation example
# ============================================================
decim_factor = 10  # Reduce from 1000 Hz to 100 Hz

# Anti-aliasing filter: cutoff at 80% of the new Nyquist (0.8 * 50 = 40 Hz)
b_aa, a_aa = signal.butter(4, (fs / decim_factor) / 2.0 * 0.8, btype='low', fs=fs)
anti_aliased = signal.filtfilt(b_aa, a_aa, noisy_signal)
decimated = anti_aliased[::decim_factor]
t_decimated = t[::decim_factor]

print(f"\n--- Decimation ---")
print(f"  Original:  {N} samples at {fs:.0f} Hz")
print(f"  Decimated: {len(decimated)} samples at {fs/decim_factor:.0f} Hz")

# ============================================================
# Plot everything
# ============================================================
fig, axes = plt.subplots(4, 1, figsize=(12, 16))

# Plot 1: Time domain comparison
ax = axes[0]
ax.plot(t, noisy_signal, 'gray', linewidth=0.5, alpha=0.5, label='Noisy signal')
ax.plot(t, true_signal, 'k-', linewidth=2, label='True signal')
ax.plot(t, ma_filtered, 'b-', linewidth=1.5, label=f'Moving avg (M={ma_window})')
ax.plot(t, butter_filtered, 'r-', linewidth=1.5, label=f'Butterworth (n={filter_order})')
ax.set_ylabel('Amplitude')
ax.set_title('Time Domain: Noisy vs Filtered')
ax.legend(loc='upper left', fontsize=9)
ax.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
ax.set_xlim([0, 0.5])  # Zoom into first 0.5s for detail

# Plot 2: Frequency domain before and after
ax = axes[1]
ax.semilogy(freqs_noisy, mag_noisy, 'gray', alpha=0.7, label='Noisy signal spectrum')
ax.semilogy(freqs_butter, mag_butter, 'r-', linewidth=1.5, label='After Butterworth')
ax.axvline(x=50, color='orange', linestyle=':', alpha=0.7, label='50 Hz power line')
ax.axvline(x=60, color='orange', linestyle='--', alpha=0.7, label='60 Hz power line')
ax.axvline(x=cutoff_hz, color='green', linestyle='-', alpha=0.7, label=f'Cutoff: {cutoff_hz} Hz')
ax.set_xlabel('Frequency (Hz)')
ax.set_ylabel('Magnitude')
ax.set_title('Frequency Domain: Noise Components Removed')
ax.legend(loc='upper right', fontsize=9)
ax.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
ax.set_xlim([0, 300])

# Plot 3: Filter frequency response
ax = axes[2]
ax.plot(w, 20 * np.log10(np.abs(h) + 1e-15), 'r-', linewidth=2,
        label=f'Order {filter_order}')
ax.plot(w8, 20 * np.log10(np.abs(h8) + 1e-15), 'b--', linewidth=2,
        label='Order 8')
ax.axvline(x=cutoff_hz, color='green', linestyle=':', label=f'Cutoff: {cutoff_hz} Hz')
ax.axhline(y=-3, color='gray', linestyle=':', alpha=0.5, label='-3 dB')
ax.set_xlabel('Frequency (Hz)')
ax.set_ylabel('Gain (dB)')
ax.set_title('Butterworth Filter Frequency Response')
ax.legend(loc='lower left', fontsize=9)
ax.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
ax.set_xlim([0, 100])
ax.set_ylim([-80, 5])

# Plot 4: Causal vs zero-phase and decimation
ax = axes[3]
ax.plot(t, true_signal, 'k-', linewidth=2, label='True signal')
ax.plot(t, butter_causal, 'b-', linewidth=1.2, alpha=0.7,
        label='Causal (lfilter, has delay)')
ax.plot(t, butter_filtered, 'r-', linewidth=1.2,
        label='Zero-phase (filtfilt, no delay)')
ax.plot(t_decimated, decimated, 'go', markersize=4,
        label=f'Decimated ({fs/decim_factor:.0f} Hz)')
ax.set_xlabel('Time (s)')
ax.set_ylabel('Amplitude')
ax.set_title('Causal vs Zero-Phase Filtering, and Decimation')
ax.legend(loc='upper left', fontsize=9)
ax.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
ax.set_xlim([0, 0.5])

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('noise_filter_designer.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
plt.show()
print("\nPlot saved: noise_filter_designer.png")
```

### Understanding the Output

<Steps>

1. **Time domain plot** (signal against time) shows the noisy ADC signal (gray), the true signal (black), and two filtered versions. The program prints the RMSE of each, the root mean square error: the typical distance from the true signal. It is 0.96 for the raw signal, 0.13 after the moving average and 0.12 after the Butterworth filter.

2. **Frequency domain plot** (signal strength against frequency) shows where the noise is: peaks at 50 Hz and 60 Hz from power lines, a peak at 200 Hz from switching noise, and white noise at all frequencies. After the Butterworth filter, almost nothing above 10 Hz is left.

3. **Filter response plot** compares the 4th-order and 8th-order Butterworth filters. The 4th-order gain falls at 80 dB per decade and the 8th-order at 160 dB per decade. The sharper filter did not give a smaller error here (0.126 against 0.123): what is left is noise below 10 Hz, which no order can remove.

4. **Causal vs zero-phase plot** shows the difference that matters most in practice. The `lfilter` output is causal: it uses only present and past samples, as a microcontroller must, so it is late. The `filtfilt` output has no delay because it passes over the recording forward and then backward, which is possible only on a finished recording.

</Steps>

## From Python to C: Implementing on a Microcontroller

<InArticleAd />


The printed coefficients can be used in C as they are. This function is the difference equation for an MCU:

```c
// IIR filter implementation for embedded systems
// Paste the b[] and a[] arrays from Python output

static double x_buf[NUM_B_COEFFS] = {0};  // input history
static double y_buf[NUM_A_COEFFS] = {0};  // output history

double iir_filter(double x_new) {
    // Shift input buffer
    for (int i = NUM_B_COEFFS - 1; i > 0; i--)
        x_buf[i] = x_buf[i - 1];
    x_buf[0] = x_new;

    // Shift output buffer
    for (int i = NUM_A_COEFFS - 1; i > 0; i--)
        y_buf[i] = y_buf[i - 1];

    // Compute new output
    double y_new = 0.0;
    for (int i = 0; i < NUM_B_COEFFS; i++)
        y_new += b[i] * x_buf[i];
    for (int i = 1; i < NUM_A_COEFFS; i++)
        y_new -= a[i] * y_buf[i];

    y_buf[0] = y_new;
    return y_new;
}
```

Call `iir_filter()` once per ADC sample in your timer interrupt. The value that it returns is the filtered reading.

### Important: Causal Filtering on MCUs

A microcontroller has the present and past samples and never the future ones, so it can run only the causal filter (the `lfilter` result). Its output is late. For the 10 Hz, 4th-order filter of this lesson, slow signals come out about 42 ms late, and a sudden step is shown to 90% after 63 ms.

Before you trust a filter in a device, check two things with `lfilter`:

- Pass a step through it and read the delay. If a control loop or an alarm cannot accept this delay, raise the cutoff or lower the order.
- Pass the shortest real event you must detect through it. If the event comes out much smaller, the filter is hiding it.

:::tip[Instrument it]
The first real signal these filters meet is often vibration. Simulate the anti-alias filter you will use, then give it a real accelerometer signal. With the sampling and the filter correct on the [device that captures the signal](/education/sensor-actuator-interfacing-stm32/), the device can [report the spectrum](/education/iot-systems/) to [siliconwit.io](https://siliconwit.io) in place of every raw sample.
:::

## Filter Design Guidelines

<InArticleAd />


| Sensor Application | Suggested Cutoff | Order | Notes |
|-------------------|-------------------|-------|-------|
| Temperature (slow) | 1 to 5 Hz | 2 | Thermal time constants are slow |
| Pressure sensor | 5 to 20 Hz | 2 to 4 | Depends on measurement speed |
| Accelerometer (tilt) | 5 to 10 Hz | 4 | Remove vibration, keep gravity |
| Current sensing | 50 to 200 Hz | 4 | Keep dynamics, remove switching noise |
| Audio preprocessing | 20 to 4000 Hz | 4 to 6 | Bandpass for voice |
| Vibration monitoring | Application specific | 4 to 8 | Often bandpass around resonance |
